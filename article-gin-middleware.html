I counted a running gin for ten minutes, and for two of them my middleware was never called

2026-07-28 10 min window / 600 requests / 5 observation points gin v1.12.0

Without changing a single line of code, I attached five observation points inside a running gin and watched for ten minutes — driving one and the same path to five different endings.

The question I was after is a dumb one: when a request comes in, how many times does my middleware chain actually get called? But this time I didn't just throw traffic at it. I made the same path, /health, produce five different endings: normal, no such route, wrong method, no token, one extra slash. Two minutes per ending, one request per second, nothing else changed in between.

Ten minutes later, those five endings read like this on the panel: four, four, four, three — and then zero. For those last two minutes the service still took 120 requests and still answered every one of them, but not a single layer of my middleware ran, and the service's own access log has nothing whatsoever to say about that stretch.

This piece isn't about a tool. It's about what counting a running framework gets you — including the part where it fooled me right at the end.

First decide where to stand

A framework's black-box quality is the same shape as an ORM's, just in a different place. An ORM puts a layer between "the line of Go you wrote" and "the SQL that actually went out"; a framework puts one between "the request the client typed" and "your handler being called". Route matching, path normalisation, redirects, the 405 decision, the 404 fallback — you can read all of it in the source, but while it runs, nothing tells you how many times each of them happened.

Standing inside your own handler is the easy option and the least useful one: the requests that never reach a handler are exactly the ones I wanted to see. Dropping down to net/http is too far the other way — there's a single entrance there and no view of where gin sent the request internally. So I stood inside gin's own routing layer and picked five positions:

PositionKindQuestion it answers
gin.(*Engine).handleHTTPRequest gin.go:708log, M={httpMethod} P={rPath}which method and path the router actually received
gin.(*Context).Next context.go:189metrichow many times the chain is re-entered
gin.serveError gin.go:765log, status={code}how many 404s and 405s
gin.redirectTrailingSlash gin.go:782metrichow many trailing-slash redirects
gin.(*Engine).ServeHTTP gin.go:667 → 674latencyhow long a request spends inside the framework, from taking a Context to handing it back

That Next counter becomes the lead of this piece. That last latency point comes back to bite me.

Two traps worth mentioning first.

The first is that a log point has to name the variables it captures — I learned that attaching this same point in an earlier round. In the scope at gin.go:708, the locals assigned further down (i, root, allowed) are not values yet; they are stack memory that hasn't entered its live range. Without a filter they get rendered too, and that round cost me several restarts. So this time I captured only httpMethod and rPath from the start. Where you attach decides not only what you can see, but what you might accidentally read.

The second is latency. On the very same ServeHTTP interval, 667 → 674, I had tried three variations two days earlier: every one reported successful registration and showed as instrumented, and the latency table stayed completely empty with a hit count of zero. This time I did exactly one thing differently — I gave the registration a name (gin_serve_latency). Same line, same interval, and the samples appeared: 602 of them, exactly 1 : 1 with the router point's 602 hits. A parameter that looks optional turns out to decide whether this whole dimension exists — a trap I find more worth writing down than the conclusion itself.

The service under observation is a very ordinary gin: gin.New(), three global middlewares (requestID → accessLog → auth), four routes and one NoRoute.

No sloppy traffic this time: one path, five endings

In an earlier round I mixed request kinds inside a single phase, and the result was "you can count what happened, but you can't attribute what it cost" — the latency on the panel was a stew. So this time the traffic is cut into five stretches, each containing exactly one kind of request, changing as close to one variable as possible:

PhaseWhat I sentEndingWhy this one
AGET /health with token200baseline: route matched, whole chain runs
BGET /nope with token404matches no route at all
CPUT /health with token405right path, wrong method
DGET /health no token401auth aborts mid-chain
EGET /health/ with token301one extra slash (and I do not follow the redirect)

Two minutes each, one request per second, 120 requests per phase. The window ran 2026-07-28T08:53:57Z to 09:04:03Z — ten minutes and six seconds. The client counted 600 requests of its own; I also hit /health twice by hand mid-window to check the service was alive, so the panel should read 602. Keep that number in mind.

Makeup off: the footprints one request leaves under five endings

At the end of the window the five points totalled: router entries 602, Next() 1,808, serveError 240, redirects 120, latency samples 602.

(The 121s in A and B each include one of my manual liveness checks.)

Four phases divide out to whole numbers: 484 ÷ 121 = 4.00, 480 ÷ 120 = 4.00, 360 ÷ 120 = 3.00. Summed, 484 + 484 + 480 + 360 + 0 = 1,808, matching the panel exactly. No request took a path I failed to account for.

First, that 4.00. For a request that matches a route, c.Next() is called four times: the engine itself calls it once at gin.go:722, and then each of my three middlewares calls it once more. That's gin's chaining design — Next() doesn't mean "run the next one", it means "run the entire rest of the chain to completion, then come back here" — so three middlewares means 1 + 3 = 4 re-entries. I knew you have to call c.Next() yourself; I had never pictured that number being exactly four for every single request.

Now look at rows B and C: 404 and 405 are also 4.00, not one call short. After gin.go:758 swaps the handlers for engine.allNoRoute, serveError runs the whole chain at gin.go:766 anyway. A request that matches no route still runs every one of your global middlewares — the auth, the rate limiter, the logging, the metrics you hung up there all run for 404s and 405s too.

That 3.00 in D is the two minutes with the token removed: auth takes c.AbortWithStatusJSON(401) and returns, and it never calls c.Next(). A whole layer's worth of re-entry disappears from the count.

All still interesting but unsurprising. The last row is where I sat up.

Those two minutes, the chain was never entered once

In phase E I changed exactly one character: /health became /health/.

gin has no such route, but it knows that dropping the slash gives one. So at gin.go:728 it calls redirectTrailingSlash, rewrites req.URL.Path in place, answers a 301, and then returns at gin.go:729 — which sits above c.Next().

Here is what those two minutes look like on the panel:

  • handleHTTPRequest: 120 (the router received every one of them)
  • redirectTrailingSlash: 120 (every one was redirected)
  • gin_serve_latency: 120 (every one really did complete ServeHTTP)
  • Next(): 0
  • serveError: 0

120 requests in, 120 responses out, and my middleware was never called once.

On the cumulative curve this needs no explanation; it just lies there:

Meanwhile the router point kept one row per second through that stretch, not one missing, rendered verbatim:

Note that rPath=/health/ — it is what the client actually typed, and gin.go:790-792 overwrites req.URL.Path in place. (Nothing downstream ran during this phase, so "everything downstream reads the rewritten path" is something I did not measure here; that part is read off the source. What I measured is that the router layer still sees the original, slash and all.) If you want to know what the client actually sent, you have to stand upstream of the rewrite.

Then the latency — the part I nearly got wrong

Five phases, one rate, one script: the latencies should be directly comparable. Here are the medians from the panel:

Phasep50Relative to A
A 2000.524 ms1.00
B 4040.613 ms1.17
C 4050.612 ms1.17
D 4010.463 ms0.88
E 3010.321 ms0.61

My reading was right there, ready-made: 404s and 405s cost 17% more than a normal request because they have to scan the other method trees as well (gin.go:738-749 really does do that); 401 is cheaper because it skips a layer; 301 is cheapest because it never enters the chain at all.

A tidy story. Tidy enough that I nearly wrote it down.

But something didn't line up. I counted how many extra probe fires each phase costs per request — excluding the latency point itself, which is present in both rounds: A 5 (one router log plus four Next), B and C 6 (one more serveError log), D 4 (only three Next), E 2 (one router log plus one redirect counter).

That ordering is identical to the latency ordering.

Which means I cannot tell whether "404 costs more" is a property of gin or the price of my own probe. In this experiment the two are perfectly collinear.

So I removed every observation point except the latency one, moved to a clean process, and ran the identical script — same 120 × 5, same one per second — for another ten minutes:

Phasep50 with five pointsp50 with latency onlyDifferenceRelative to A (clean)
A 2000.524 ms0.119 ms0.4051.00
B 4040.613 ms0.104 ms0.5090.87
C 4050.612 ms0.065 ms0.5470.55
D 4010.463 ms0.072 ms0.3910.60
E 3010.321 ms0.020 ms0.3010.17

The ordering flipped completely.

In a clean gin, a 404 is 13% cheaper than a normal request and a 405 is 45% cheaper — the exact opposite of the reasoning above. That "17% more expensive" I was looking at is the weight of the log point I hung on serveError.

The 45% on the 405 I can account for: gin.go:751 swaps the handlers for engine.allNoMethod, and this demo never registers a NoMethod handler, so that chain runs out with no handler at the end — the actual response is a single line of plain text, default405Body, written by serveError. Whereas /health has to encode JSON.

As for why a 404 is 13% cheaper than a 200, I have no answer I can verify. This demo's NoRoute is my own and calls c.JSON just like /health does, so the convenient "404 skips the JSON encoding" story doesn't hold. I measured the gap, 0.104 against 0.119; I did not measure a mechanism, so I'm not inventing one for the reader.

The same table shows something else: observation made every request 0.30 to 0.55 ms more expensive, which is 77% to 94% of the number the panel displayed. Phase E is the extreme case — gin itself spends 0.020 ms, the panel said 0.321 ms, sixteen times over, because that request is so cheap to begin with that the probes' fixed cost swamps it entirely.

What's left of phase E — the part that really belongs to gin — is 0.020 ms, one sixth of a normal request (0.119 ÷ 0.020 = 6.0). And the distribution is tight: p25 to p90 spans 0.013 to 0.054 ms, and the largest sample in the whole phase is 0.097 ms — it never even breaks 0.1.

A request that gin redirects on the spot costs a sixth of a normal one, and will not appear in any log you have.

Honest part: what I got wrong, can't see, and can't call

77% to 94% of the latency numbers in my first round were me. That's the section above. The point isn't "so latency observation is useless" — another round with the same tool is what produced the truth — it's that when your observation points sit on a path every request takes, what you measure is "the observed system", not "the system". If phases happen to trigger different numbers of probes, that difference will impersonate the conclusion you were hoping for. Without counting the probe fires and running a second round, this piece would have contained a very tidy, completely wrong causal claim.

One round of evidence got eaten. On the first attempt at the control run, the observation host died silently 2 minutes 13 seconds in: the gin under observation was perfectly fine, the traffic script ran its full ten minutes and printed a beautiful complete tally at the end — but the panel only kept the first phase's 121 samples, the other four phases' 480 samples were gone, and the log file contained not one line of error. I only caught it because the sample count didn't match the client's count. The comparison table above is from a full re-run. The lesson is blunt: if you don't reconcile after a window closes, you have no idea whether you're holding a fifth of your data.

The control round carries 3% contamination. For that round I had a health check polling /health every 30 seconds — precisely to detect the failure above — so each phase contains roughly 4 extra "normal" requests, 124 samples instead of 120. They look identical to everything else in the sample set; I couldn't pick them out, so I didn't. That round's panel also shows three orphan spikes, one each in phases A, B and C (maxima of 45.2, 44.9 and 35.1 ms), which drag those averages to 0.36–0.48 ms — which is why I quote medians and quartiles above, never averages.

The Next() counter can't tell you which layer went missing. It only tells me how many times the chain was re-entered. Phase D drops to 3.00 and I know one call is gone, but "the missing one is auth" comes from 4 − 3 = 1 plus reading the code — the panel never pointed at it. To have the panel say so directly, you'd need a point on each middleware.

Phase boundaries carry about one row of attribution error. The service's own access log has second-resolution timestamps, so a row or two at each boundary lands in the neighbouring phase (one of phase A's 121 rows is a 404). The totals are exact; the per-phase edges are approximate.

Everything here ran at one request per second. I did not measure high load, so nothing in this piece is a statement about throughput. Whether these ratios hold at thousands of requests per second I don't know, and I'm not going to guess.

What you can see, you can see; what you can't, you mark as unseen — you don't hand the reader something that pretends to be complete.

The comparison: why the service's own access log isn't enough

This service already carries an accessLog middleware printing the most typical thing there is: method, path, status, elapsed. In fairness it is not blind at all — over the same window it recorded 481 rows: 120 each for 404, 405 and 401, and 121 for 200 (including my manual liveness checks). Every cell matches what the client counted.

The problem is that the router on the panel received 602.

A gap of 121. 120 of those are phase E's redirects; the remaining one is the boundary error above. In the whole access log, 301 appears 0 times and /health/ appears 0 times — not "logged more coarsely", but a structural blank two minutes wide.

The reason is in the earlier table: on the redirect path Next() is hit 0 times, and accessLog is itself a layer of that chain. Nobody calls it, so it doesn't run; it doesn't run, so there is no row. The same log proves the other half as well: for the 404 and 405 phases it missed nothing, recording 120 rows each, precisely because the chain really did run. What it says proves 404s run the full chain; what it doesn't say proves 301s ran none of it.

And even where it does print, the elapsed time it prints is not the elapsed time you think it is. Put its own numbers next to the clean round's ServeHTTP:

PhaseAccess log medianWhat the framework actually spent (clean round p50)How much it can see
A 2000.043 ms0.119 ms36%
C 4050.0006 ms0.065 ms1%
E 301— (no such row)0.020 ms0%

That 0.0006 ms on the 405 is the interesting one (a median — around six hundred nanoseconds; every row in that stretch is a few hundred). accessLog times around its own call to c.Next(), and on the 405 path c.Next() runs engine.allNoMethod — which, since this demo never registers NoMethod, contains only my three middlewares and no handler at all. The actual 405 response is written inside serveError, after c.Next() has already returned (gin.go:766-778). So almost nothing happens inside the stretch it times: your log says this request took 0.0006 ms while the framework spent 0.065 — it sees one percent.

So what did the observation points add? Three things:

  1. They stand outside the chain. The access log only records requests where the chain ran; the router point records requests the framework received. The gap between those two numbers is exactly the volume handled before the chain ever starts.
  2. They give you the verbatim, pre-rewrite content. Those 120 M=GET P=/health/ rows are what req.URL.Path looked like before it was overwritten.
  3. They reconcile. 602 against 481 is itself information. The day that gap starts widening, a batch of requests is being handled at the framework layer — and your log will be entirely silent about it.

Wrapping up

I didn't change a line of code, didn't restart the service, didn't stand up a trace backend first. I picked five positions in a running process, drove one path to five endings, and counted carefully for ten minutes — and then, because the numbers looked too tidy, pulled the points off and ran it again.

What that bought: a request re-entering the same function four times; 404s and 405s running the entire middleware chain with nothing skipped; that number dropping from 4.00 to 3.00 the moment auth aborts, dividing evenly across three phases; two minutes in which 120 requests never got through the chain's door while the service's own log said nothing about them; the router layer still seeing the /health/ the client typed; that redirected request costing a sixth of a normal one; and my own observation points impersonating a "404s cost 17% more" conclusion that wasn't there.

A framework is a black box, but a black box is only "invisible by default", not "impossible to see". And once you can see it, there's a second job: make sure what you're looking at is it, and not you.
