I removed my own probes, and the 55-millisecond tail did not move

2026-09-0610 min 11 s window / 601 requests / 5 probesgin v1.12.0

📊 The numbers here come from this run’s remote dashboard: gin-middleware-20260906

A gin service, one request a second, ten minutes. A tenth of the requests took 55 milliseconds; the rest finished under 1 — and there is almost nothing in between. This is not about tooling. It is about what watching a running thing gets you.

Deciding where to stand

The black-box part of a framework is this: r.Use(requestID, accessLog, auth) registers three middlewares — but that is a fact about startup. Once it is running, how many times a request actually advances that chain is something nothing will tell you.

The easy place to stand is inside my own handler. I ruled it out quickly: the requests that never reach a handler are exactly the ones I wanted to see — 404, 405, 401, 301, each of them finished inside the framework.

So the probes went on the framework itself: the ServeHTTP entry (take the request, and time that span), the route match in handleHTTPRequest (decide the route), Context.Next() (advance the chain), and redirectTrailingSlash.

Traffic runs in five phases, 120 requests each, one per second, changing exactly one variable at a time: GET /health 200, GET /nope 404, PUT /health 405, no token for 401, trailing slash for 301. Same rhythm, five endings.

Only one thing was predictable up front: if the chain is never cut short, the ratio should be ×3. Here is what was not — the panel's p95 is 54.839 ms and its p50 is 0.821. Same path, 54.839 ÷ 0.821 = 66.8 times apart. Why?

What ten minutes showed

The ratios landed first: 601 requests, 1,803 advances, 1803 ÷ 601 = 3. And 120 trailing-slash redirects, 120 ÷ 601 = 0.2. Both numbers are on the graph; neither is an estimate.

Context.Next() came back with eight stack shapes, and they draw the chain directly (prefixes and .func1 elided):

handleHTTPRequest · Next                                        240
handleHTTPRequest · Next · requestID · Next                     240
handleHTTPRequest · Next · requestID · Next · accessLog · Next   240
handleHTTPRequest · Next · requestID · Next · accessLog · Next · auth · Next   120

serveError · Next                                               241
serveError · Next · requestID · Next                            241
serveError · Next · requestID · Next · accessLog · Next         241
serveError · Next · requestID · Next · accessLog · Next · auth · Next          240

One Next(), four depths — nothing above it the first time, three middlewares deep the last. The eight add up to 241×3 + 240×4 + 120 = 1,803, exactly the advance count.

And the fourth line of the top half reads 120 while the first three read 240. The phases that go through handleHTTPRequest are the 200 and the 401, 120 requests each. They both reach the first three depths; only the 200 reaches the fourth. In the 401 phase auth cuts the chain, so the call it would have made downward never happens.

The cut does not need measuring. It is the stack that is missing.

As for that 66.8-fold gap, spreading the 601 samples out shows it is not a long tail. It is two hills:

  0–5  ms : 513
  5–10 ms :  24
 10–15 ms :   1
 15–20 ms :   1
 25–30 ms :   1
 50–55 ms :  49
 55–60 ms :  12

The middle is empty. 61 requests take 55 milliseconds, 513 finish inside 5, and there is almost no transition between them.

I thought that was me

It was a reasonable guess: my probes sit on the chain, every request goes through them, and 55 milliseconds is exactly the order of magnitude of "something is in the way". The last time I wrote about this service, that is what I wrote.

When the window closed I removed the three probes on the chain — keeping only the two at the entry — and ran the identical script over the same five phases again.

It did not move.

                        requests   >50ms   share    p50
main window                 601      61    10.1%   0.821ms
chain probes removed        300      31    10.3%   0.814ms

Both peaks are still there, in the same proportion. That 55 milliseconds is not something I brought with me.

Split by phase, where it lives is clear:

phase                    requests   >50ms
GET /health   200            120      19
GET /nope     404            120      16
PUT /health   405            120      11
GET /health   401            120      15
GET /health/  301            120       0

Only the 301 phase has none. And it is the only one that returns before the middleware chain starts — when redirectTrailingSlash takes it, Next() has not been called once.

Being honest about it

I do not know what those 55 milliseconds are. I know they are not my probes, and I know they travel with the full request path rather than the one that returns early. Going further needs probes this round did not place, and what was not measured does not get written.

The control ran once. 10.1% against 10.3% is a gap small enough to call "no difference", but once is once — on this very service I have run a single control before, drawn a conclusion, and had a second run overturn it. The evidence here is harder (same distribution shape, 0.2 of a percentage point apart), but it is still one run.

The 301 phase is missing more than the chain — it is also missing the probes on it. So "no slow requests there" fits both explanations. What separates them is the table above: chain still running, probes gone, still 10.3%.

And one request was not mine. The route column carries a single M=GET P=/index_zh_TW.html — a stale browser tab of my own, hitting the port the demo now holds. No token, no such route, so it went NoRoute → serveError and was cut by auth. That is why three of the serveError stacks read 241 and the deepest reads 240. 601 instead of 600; that one is the difference.

The result

I now know that the tail pulling p95 up to 54.839 ms is not a mark left by the observation. It was already there — and I would have written it as the former, because that explanation was both plausible and comfortable.

Taking my own probes off and running it again cost five minutes. It turned one sentence from "I think" into "I know" — or more precisely, into a smaller conclusion that actually holds: I do not know what those 55 milliseconds are, but I know they are not me.
