I watched a running cache for eighteen minutes and saw it throw away 86% of my writes, returning true every single time

2026-08-01 18 min window / 110,814 Set calls / 8 observation points dgraph-io/ristretto v2.4.2

The other day I did something I don't normally do: without changing a single line of code, I attached a handful of observation points inside the cache of a running Go process, and then just sat there watching for eighteen minutes.

In those eighteen minutes the program wrote to the cache 110,572 times, and got true every single time. What the panel says is: 95,879 of those were thrown away on the spot in another goroutine, never stored at all.

This piece isn't about a tool. It's about the act of watching something while it runs — because this behaviour is readable in the source. I have read it. I even knew it was called an admission policy. But I had never actually seen the ratio, and I had never seen what it compares when it makes that decision.

First, decide where to stand

A cache is a different kind of black box than an ORM. With an ORM the question is "what SQL does my line of code turn into" — at least you know something goes out at the end. With a cache the question is "does the line I wrote count for anything at all" — and its API never intended to tell you.

Cache.Set is declared as func (c *Cache[K, V]) Set(key K, value V, cost int64) bool. It returns a bool. The docs are honest about it: that bool means "was it put into the buffer", not "was it stored". Whether it is actually admitted happens later, after another goroutine pulls it back out of that buffer.

So the first decision when instrumenting is: which layer do you stand on.

Stand inside Set and all you see is true — that's the stamp on the envelope at the front desk, and it has nothing to do with what happens afterwards. Stand at the layer that actually decides (defaultPolicy.Add) and you see admitted-or-not, but not who, not when, not why this particular write happened.

What I settled on was instrumenting both layers and letting the two sets of numbers reconcile against each other:

  • The entrance: cache.go:357, the return true line of Set — the number of times a caller was told true.
  • The entrance's two other exits: cache.go:352 (the key is already in the store, so it becomes an update) and cache.go:365 (the buffer is full, return false). I put a point on each of those too, because I need to know they didn't happen before I can claim the entrance has only one shape.
  • The four exits of the deciding layer: policy.go:116 (room available, admit directly), policy.go:166 (admit after evicting someone), policy.go:107 (the policy already has this key), policy.go:148 (reject).
  • The rejection point doesn't just count — it also pulls out three variables as they are at that moment: cost={cost} incHits={incHits} minHits={minHits}.

minHits is the field that turns out to be the lead character of this whole piece.

One trap worth mentioning up front: the receivers of these functions are *Cache[K, V] and *defaultPolicy[V], and methods on generic types are not called that in the compiled binary at all. Go does GC shape stenciling, so the binary holds (*Cache[go.shape.string,go.shape.string]).SetWithTTL, and the /v2 major-version path element wedges itself between the package name and the receiver on top of that. Between the name you derive from source and the name in the binary sit two layers of translation — I spent an hour fighting names before I saw my first number. None of this has anything to do with Ristretto, but it was the most real part of this run.

I deliberately split the load in two. For the first 180 rounds, a key space of 2,000 (twice the capacity); from round 181 on, a key space of 50,000 (fifty times the capacity). The calling side has no idea any of this happened — same program, same line of Set, same process, no restart, no config change in between.

What the first thirteen minutes showed: one Set with its makeup off

The observation window is 2026-07-31 17:04:06.463Z to 17:22:04.266Z — seventeen minutes fifty-eight seconds, 245 rounds of 500 operations each.

Start with phase A (the first 180 rounds, key space 2,000). The line of Go I wrote is just this:

if cache.Set(key, "value-"+key, 1) {
    t.setTrue++
}

What the panel says is actually running is this:

There are three things in this table that I "knew" but had never actually seen.

The entrance has only one shape. The two points at cache.go:352 and :365 never fired once in eighteen minutes. So in this run Set never took the "actually an update" path, and the buffer was never full — all 82,728 trues at the entrance mean exactly the same thing: the thing made it into the queue. That is the foundation for everything that follows, and it rests on two points that read zero from beginning to end.

The books balance. The four exits of the deciding layer sum to 21 + 17 + 14,765 + 67,926 = 82,729; the entrance is 82,728. Off by one — that's the one still in flight in the queue at the instant I took the snapshot. Eighteen minutes, eighty thousand-odd operations, off by one.

The cost I passed is not the cost the policy weighs. I passed 1 every time. But the cost captured at the rejection point is 57 on every single one of its 95,821 rows. Before handing an item to the policy, Ristretto quietly adds itemSize — the size of the internal storeItem struct itself (56 bytes on amd64). So I thought I was saying "this thing is worth 1 unit"; what the policy heard was "57".

And that number can be checked on the spot: a capacity of 1,000 divided by 57 is 17.5, so before it starts evicting anyone, this cache holds exactly 17 of my items. The hit count of the "room available, admitted directly" point (policy.go:116) over the full eighteen minutes is precisely 17. A constant I never passed and a count I never expected, meeting on the panel.

All of that is interesting but not surprising. What made me sit up was the content captured at the rejection point.

incHits=3, minHits=16: a contest it never told you about

The template on the rejection line, copied verbatim from the panel:

cost=57 | incHits=3 | minHits=16
cost=57 | incHits=2 | minHits=16
cost=57 | incHits=0 | minHits=16

incHits is the estimated heat of the key I am about to write. minHits is the heat of the coldest one it drew after randomly sampling the current residents. The code is this:

if incHits < minHits {
    p.metrics.add(rejectSets, key, 1)
    return victims, false
}

Which is to say: on every Set, your data has to win a contest against something already living inside. Lose and it is dropped on the spot — while the caller receives true.

Across those 95,821 rows, incHits < minHits holds 100% of the time — not one exception. That isn't a surprise (it is the condition on that line), but it does prove the variables I captured are the ones the decision uses, not something else.

The real finding is in the distribution of minHits. Of phase A's 67,911 rejections, 57,881 (85.2%) have a minHits of exactly 16.

16 is not a coincidence. Ristretto's frequency estimator is a Count-Min sketch of 4-bit counters (every counter in sketch.go is masked with & 0x0f, so it tops out at 15), plus 1 when the doorkeeper bloom filter hits. 16 is the largest value this ruler can express.

So what those 57,881 rejections really mean is not "your data wasn't hot enough" — it's
"the resident it happened to draw is already pinned at the counter's ceiling, and whether it has been touched 16 times or 16,000 times is something this ruler cannot measure."

Phase A's mean incHits is 3.699; its mean minHits is 15.127. A challenger averaging 3.7 points against a field averaging 15.1, 85% of whom are already at a perfect score. The contest isn't hard to win — the ruler is too short.

Round 181: I widened the key space 25×, and the caller never noticed a thing

At round 181 the program switched the key space from 2,000 to 50,000. No restart, no config change, not one character of that Set line touched.

What the caller saw: still 100% true. Phase B's 28,086 Set calls, 28,086 trues.

An API whose return value is identical throughout, with behaviour underneath shifting by an order of magnitude, while the bool in the caller's hand looks exactly the same from start to finish.

The minHits distribution flipped over completely too. Of phase B's 27,910 rejections, the number with minHits equal to 16 is zero:

Mean minHits dropped from 15.127 to 9.098; mean incHits from 3.699 to 1.375. The residents are no longer at a perfect score — widen the key space 25× and the same sketch has 25× as much to remember, so every key's share of the counters is diluted.

Yet the admission rate got worse, because the challengers fell harder: from an average of 3.7 down to 1.4. Both sides got colder, and the challenger cooled faster.

The honest part: three things I got wrong in this run

First, I can't call that "86%" a disaster. This is a cache; throwing things away is its job. Something with a capacity of 1,000 facing 50,000 distinct keys and discarding 99.5% is correct behaviour, not a bug. The point of this piece is not "Ristretto has a problem", it's "that true didn't tell you any of this". What actually bites is someone treating if !cache.Set(...) as error handling, or assuming a Get after a Set will hit.

Second, the latency I measured was mostly me. I put a latency interval on policy.go:122 → 168 to see how long the eviction path takes. It collected 14,901 samples: p50 4.537 ms, p95 7.893, p99 9.885. Those numbers cannot be used as Ristretto performance data, for two reasons: that interval contains another one of my own observation points (policy.go:166), and the whole program ran 4.75× slower under observation. A p50 of 4.5 ms is roughly the cost of one observation-point hit — what I measured was the observer, not the observed. So apart from this paragraph, no millisecond figures appear anywhere in this piece.

Third, half the panel's Metrics tab is noise. When a metric point is given no template, it pulls out every variable in scope — which is why the panel grew series nobody wants to look at, such as conflictHash, keyHash, ~r0 and expiration. That is a consequence of my not setting a variable filter when I attached the points, not of the data being wrong; every count in this article comes from each point's exact hit count, not from those charts. I'm attaching that tab as-is below, because it is what this run actually looked like.

So what about a control? I ran one round with the same binary and the same parameters, with no observation points attached at all:

Observation made the program 4.75× slower, and that has to be said plainly. But the two numbers this article rests on — "100% true" and the rejection count — differ by only 0.8% between the two. The added column differs more, and you can see why: the control runs faster, the policy goroutine can't keep up, and roughly 4,500 items were still stuck in the queue unprocessed at the instant of sampling. In the observed round the producer was slowed down, so the books happen to balance better.

A comparison: why Ristretto's own metrics still aren't enough

To be fair, Ristretto does have built-in metrics, and they are not blind. m.SetsRejected() is the rejection count; m.KeysAdded() is the admission count. I reconciled against them throughout:

  • The observation points counted 17 + 14,898 = 14,915 admissions; Ristretto itself says 14,914.
  • The observation points counted 95,879 rejections; Ristretto itself says 95,637 (two samples thirteen seconds apart).
  • SetsDropped() is 0 throughout, consistent with my two points that never fired.

Three mutually independent sources pointing at the same set of numbers. So what did the observation points add?

  1. They don't tell you Add has four exits. KeysAdded lumps "room available, admitted directly" (17) and "admitted after evicting someone" (14,898) into the same bucket; and the policy.go:107 path, "the policy already has this key" (21), increments neither counter — it is invisible in Ristretto's own ledger. I only know it happened 21 times because I put a point on that line.
  2. They give you the outcome, not the reason. SetsRejected = 95,637 tells you how many were rejected, not who they lost to or by how much. Something like incHits=3 / minHits=16 exists in no metric anywhere, because nobody decided in advance to export it — and that 16 is the actual finding of this piece.
  3. They don't reconcile. An entrance of 110,814 against four exits summing to 110,815: that "off by one" is itself information. The day those two numbers diverge sharply, a batch of Set calls is disappearing before it ever reaches the policy. Built-in metrics can't give you that, because they have no concept of an "entrance" at all.

Wrapping up

I changed no code, recompiled no Ristretto, restarted no process. I just picked eight positions in a running process, attached observation points, widened the key space 25× partway through, and watched properly for eighteen minutes.

What that bought: I saw that the true from Set only means "it made it into the queue", and that the queue has four exits behind it; saw the cost=1 I passed become 57 in the policy's hands, so that a cache with capacity 1,000 really holds 17 of my items; saw 95,879 of 110,814 trues thrown away on the spot; saw that the reason for the discard is a contest between incHits and minHits, and that in phase A 85% of the opponents were already pinned at the ceiling of a 4-bit counter; saw the admission rate fall from 17.87% to 0.47% the moment the key space widened, while the bool in the caller's hand never changed by a single character.

A cache is a black box, but a black box is only "invisible by default", not "impossible to see". That true was lying the whole time — it was just very polite about it. And what it was lying about should have been visible all along.
