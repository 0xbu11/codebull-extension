One Put arrives, three goroutines each finish part of it

2026-08-286m40s window / 31,269 Puts / 6 probesetcd v3.8 single node

Probes on a running etcd, from the gRPC entry down to the WAL save and the state apply — no code change, six minutes forty.

Why these six places, and why two kinds of traffic

An etcd write does not travel in one straight line: gRPC takes the request, it becomes a raft proposal, it hits the disk, it is applied to state — and each of those may run on a different goroutine. You can read the go func() in the source; you cannot read "how many disk writes per Put".

So six probes are laid along the whole write path: kvServer.Put, EtcdServer.Put, processInternalRaftRequestOnce, wal.Save, and applyEntryNormal.

The traffic is deliberately in two halves: one write per second from a single client, then eight clients flat out. Same requests, two ways of arriving — to see whether the ratios and the latency move with them. The expectation was the intuitive one: busier should mean slower.

What each signal says here

metric (how often) — the request line holds ×1 throughout: 31,269 → 31,257 → 31,240. But the WAL save is 33,544, more than the proposals, and the apply is 31,214, slightly fewer. Three numbers that do not match, which is itself the statement that these stages are not synchronous.

latency (how long) — on the wal.Save interval, and this is the centre of the piece. First half p50 5.9ms; second half 0.45ms. More requests, and a single save got thirteen times faster — several proposals batched into one fsync. Drag the timeline into either half and the number becomes that half's.

There is a further signal in the difference between metric and latency: the counter says the function was entered 33,544 times, the latency probe measured 22,120. The missing eleven thousand are empty HardStates — in the function, short-circuited before fsync. The two signals are not measuring the same event: one counts arrivals, the other counts completions.

flamegraph (from where) — five frame chains, and this column answers "why is the graph three islands" directly:

Take the Put    goexit · serveStreams · +9 · _KV_Put_Handler · Put
WAL save        goexit · raftNode.start · storage.Save · wal.Save
Apply an entry  goexit · NewFIFOScheduler · fifo.run · +5 · applyEntryNormal

Three different runtime.goexits, three paths. A stack taken after go func() carries nothing from the caller, so the graph does not draw an edge it never measured.

What came out

The three islands are not something the graph missed; they are the shape of the program — and the stack column names all three goroutines, so a reader can verify that sentence instead of having to trust it.

As for latency, the up-front expectation that busier means slower was wrong. Batching amortises the fsync, so the heavier the load the cheaper each individual save. That is not derivable from the source; it has to be measured.
